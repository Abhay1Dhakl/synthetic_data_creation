Introduction
Diabetes occurs when blood glucose, also called blood sugar, is too high.
High blood glucose can cause health problems over time.
The main types of diabetes are type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and gestational diabetes.

Symptoms and Causes
Common symptoms include increased thirst, frequent urination, feeling tired, unexplained weight loss, and blurred vision.
Many people have no symptoms and do not know they have diabetes.
Each type of diabetes has different causes.

Risk Factors
The risk of type 2 diabetes is higher in people with a family history of diabetes.
Risk is also higher in people who are overweight or have obesity.
Physical inactivity increases risk.
Age, race, ethnicity, and certain health problems also affect risk.
A history of gestational diabetes is a risk factor for both the parent and the child.

Prevention
Type 2 diabetes can sometimes be prevented or delayed.
Weight loss can help if a person is overweight.
Being active for 30 minutes on most days of the week can reduce risk.
A reduced-calorie eating plan may help prevent type 2 diabetes.
Some people also take metformin to help prevent type 2 diabetes.

Tests and Diagnosis
Doctors use different tests to diagnose diabetes and prediabetes.
The A1C test shows average blood glucose over the past 3 months.
An A1C from 5.7% to 6.4% is used for prediabetes screening and diagnosis.
An A1C of 6.5% or higher can support a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and usually needs repeat confirmation.
Doctors may choose different tests depending on symptoms or pregnancy status.

Management
Managing diabetes means managing blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels.
Not smoking, leading a healthy lifestyle, and taking medicines are important parts of care.
People with diabetes should work with their healthcare team to create a care plan that works for them.
For many people, the A1C goal is below 7%, but personal goals may differ.

Medicines and Treatment
Treatment may include diabetes pills, insulin, or other shots.
Some people also need medicines for related health problems.
Some types of surgery may be an option for selected patients.
The diabetes ABCs focus on A1C, blood pressure, and cholesterol.

Healthy Living
A diabetes meal plan includes attention to what a person eats, how much they eat, and when they eat.
Moderate physical activity on most days of the week helps control blood glucose.
Healthy living can help prevent complications that affect the heart, kidneys, eyes, and brain.
If a person has overweight or obesity, losing even a modest amount of weight may improve blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol.

Hypoglycemia
Low blood glucose is common in people with type 1 diabetes and in many people with type 2 diabetes who use insulin or certain medicines.
Hypoglycemia can cause shakiness, hunger, tiredness, dizziness, confusion, irritability, or a fast heartbeat.
Very low blood glucose can cause seizures or loss of consciousness and needs treatment right away.
People at risk for severe hypoglycemia may need a glucagon kit and medical alert identification.

Kidney Disease
Diabetic kidney disease often has no symptoms.
Testing is the only way to know whether diabetic kidney disease is present.
Doctors use blood and urine tests to check for diabetic kidney disease.
People with type 2 diabetes and people with type 1 diabetes for more than 5 years should be tested every year for kidney disease.
Reaching blood glucose and blood pressure goals is one of the best ways to slow or prevent diabetes-related kidney disease.
Stopping smoking, limiting salt, staying active, and maintaining a healthy weight can help protect the kidneys.

Complications and Counseling
Diabetes can affect almost every part of the body.
Complications may involve the heart, nerves, feet, kidneys, eyes, gums, and bladder.
Smoking and tobacco products can make diabetes problems worse, especially heart disease and foot problems.
People who smoke should be encouraged to stop and seek help if needed.
