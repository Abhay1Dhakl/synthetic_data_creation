# Chronic Kidney Disease

## Block 1

Source title: Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) - NIDDK  
Source URL: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd

Important extracted text:

Chronic kidney disease means the kidneys are damaged or have a structural problem and cannot filter blood the way they should.

Major risk factors include diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, and family history of kidney disease.

In adults, diabetes and high blood pressure are the most common causes of CKD.

Testing may be the only way to know that kidney disease is present.

People with diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, or family history of kidney failure should be checked.

The sooner kidney disease is found, the sooner treatment can begin.

Healthy habits and blood pressure control are important parts of kidney protection.

## Block 2

Source title: Managing Chronic Kidney Disease - NIDDK  
Source URL: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd/managing

Important extracted text:

The most important step in treating kidney disease is controlling blood pressure.

For many people, a blood pressure goal of less than 140/90 mm Hg is used.

Ten management areas highlighted in the source include:

- Control blood pressure
- Meet blood glucose goals if diabetes is present
- Monitor kidney health with the care team
- Take medicines as prescribed
- Work with a dietitian
- Be physically active
- Aim for a healthy weight
- Get enough sleep
- Stop smoking
- Find healthy ways to cope with stress and depression

Kidney monitoring includes tracking GFR and urine albumin over time.

Patients are encouraged to ask about test results, diet changes, medicine timing, worsening disease, referral to a nephrologist, and future dialysis or transplant planning.

## Block 3

Source title: Preventing Chronic Kidney Disease - NIDDK  
Source URL: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd/prevention

Important extracted text:

Risk of kidney disease is higher in people with diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, or a family history of kidney failure.

Early kidney disease may not cause symptoms, so testing can be the only way to know whether the kidneys are healthy.

Kidney protection includes preventing or controlling conditions that damage the kidneys.

Important prevention measures include:

- Choosing heart-healthy foods
- Reducing salt and added sugars
- Staying physically active
- Seeking treatment quickly for urinary tract infection

The source recommends aiming for less than 2,300 milligrams of sodium per day.

The DASH eating plan may help lower blood pressure and support kidney health.

## Block 4

Source title: Healthy Eating for Adults with Chronic Kidney Disease - NIDDK  
Source URL: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd/healthy-eating-adults-chronic-kidney-disease

Important extracted text:

When kidneys are damaged, food and fluid choices affect salt and mineral balance in the body.

Healthy eating can help the kidneys stay healthier longer and may prevent or delay some CKD-related problems.

As CKD advances, nutritional needs may change, and some patients may need help from a registered dietitian.

Important nutrition areas include:

- Liquids
- Phosphorus
- Potassium
- Protein
- Sodium

Too much sodium can cause fluid retention, swelling, and higher blood pressure, which increases stress on the kidneys and heart.

Phosphorus buildup can weaken bones and harm blood vessels.

Potassium that is too low or too high can cause heart and muscle problems.

Protein intake may need balance because excess waste from protein can strain damaged kidneys, while too little protein can lead to malnutrition.
